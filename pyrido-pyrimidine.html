Pyrido[1,2-a]pyrimidin-4-one

Structure, history, synthesis, and applications of the ring system — established by H. Antaki and V. Petrow, 1951; characterised by ultraviolet spectroscopy, 1958–1962

In brief: The pyrido[1,2-a]pyrimidin-4-one ring system was structurally established by H. Antaki and V. Petrow in 1951 (J. Chem. Soc., 551–555; DOI: 10.1039/jr9510000551), correcting a misassignment that had stood in the literature since 1911. That correction was not an isolated project. It arose within a broader programme, begun in Antaki's 1950 doctoral thesis, in which a common condensation and ring-closure strategy was tested across five related cyclic amidine / 2-aminoheterocycle systems. The pyrido branch produced the structural correction and was then developed further by Antaki in synthesis, ultraviolet spectroscopy, and biological investigation; the four sibling branches acquired their own later histories in academic and pharmaceutical chemistry.

The 1951 Structural Correction and Its Broader Chemical Implications

The structural correction is easiest to understand in the context of the programme that produced it. Antaki's 1950 thesis and the 1951 Antaki–Petrow paper did not begin with five unrelated target molecules. They applied a common condensation and ring-closure strategy — centred on ethyl β-aminocrotonate and cyclic amidines / 2-aminoheterocycles — across a set of related heterocyclic partners. The result was a family of parallel fused-pyrimidine branches. The pyrido[1,2-a]pyrimidin-4-one branch was the one in which the work also resolved the long-standing 2-oxo / 4-oxo structural problem.

How to read the tree. These five systems are sibling branches of one synthetic programme. The thiazolo, benzothiazolo, benzoxazolo and benzimidazole systems are not chemical descendants of pyrido[1,2-a]pyrimidin-4-one. The structural correction belongs specifically to the pyrido branch; the breadth lies in the common strategy and the several independent lineages that followed.

Terminology. The chemical literature uses several related forms for this ring system, including pyrido[1,2-a]pyrimidine, pyrido[1,2-a]pyrimidin-4-one, 4H-pyrido[1,2-a]pyrimidin-4-one, pyrido[1,2-a]pyrimidinone, and the broader plural pyridopyrimidines. This page concerns the historical development of the 4-one ring system — its structural history, spectroscopic characterisation, and subsequent applications.

What It Is

The pyrido[1,2-a]pyrimidin-4-one is a fused bicyclic ring system in which a pyridine ring and a pyrimidinone ring share a bridgehead nitrogen atom. It is described in the medicinal-chemistry literature as a privileged scaffold — a molecular framework that, decorated in different ways, yields compounds active against many unrelated biological targets. Today its derivatives appear in marketed medicines spanning psychiatry, allergy, analgesia, and rare genetic disease.

The Broader Synthetic Programme behind the Correction

Antaki's doctoral thesis — Part I, "Synthesis of Heterocyclic Compounds Containing the Condensed Pyrimidine Ring System" — was organised around five related systems developed through a common condensation and ring-closure strategy. The unifying idea was not that all five final scaffolds were variants of pyrido[1,2-a]pyrimidin-4-one. Rather, different cyclic amidines / 2-aminoheterocycles were used to test how broadly the same annulation chemistry could be applied.

In the 1951 paper, Antaki and Petrow described ethyl β-aminocrotonate as "markedly superior" to ethyl acetoacetate for this class of condensation. Applying it across different heterocyclic partners produced the azaquinolizine / pyrido[1,2-a]pyrimidinone system together with diazathianaphthene, diazathiafluorene, diazaoxafluorene, and diazacarbazole chemistry. Several of the resulting fused systems were described by Antaki as previously unknown. Their later histories are documented on the Fused Pyrimidine Ring Systems page.

The pyrido branch became especially important because the same programme also resolved a structural problem that had remained unsettled for nearly forty years.

The Pyrido Structural Correction (1951)

In 1911, Palazzo and Tamburini prepared the first compound in the series but assigned it the wrong structure — the 2-oxo isomer rather than the 4-oxo. The same incorrect assignment was repeated by Seide (1925), Crippa and Scevola (1937), and Khitrik (1939), and stood uncorrected for nearly forty years across four independent research groups.

In 1951, H. Antaki and V. Petrow resolved it. By an independent and unambiguous synthesis — reacting 2-bromopyridine with ethyl β-aminocrotonate — they demonstrated that the correct structure was the 4-oxo isomer. The canonical review of the ring system describes this as an "unequivocal synthesis" and records that the products of this chemistry were "first described by Antaki" (Hermecz and Mészáros, Advances in Heterocyclic Chemistry, Vol. 33, 1983, pp. 241–330; DOI).

The assignment was confirmed independently within a year by Adams and Pachter (J. Am. Chem. Soc. 1952, 74, 5491–5497; DOI), who synthesised the parent 2-one and 4-one, recorded their ultraviolet spectra as reference standards, and concluded on that basis that the Antaki and Petrow product was the 4-one. It was relied upon by later workers and confirmed again by independent modern methods: Yale and colleagues at the Squibb Institute for Medical Research (J. Heterocyclic Chem. 1973, 10, 123; DOI) repeated the reactions and found their products "identical in m.p., ir, uv, and pmr spectra… as described by Antaki and Petrow," verifying the 4-one structures by X-ray crystallography and NMR.

Seen in isolation, this is an important structural correction. Seen within the programme represented above, it is also one outcome of a broader synthetic investigation that established or extended several fused-pyrimidine systems from a common methodological starting point.

The Ultraviolet Record (1958–1962)

Having established the structure, Antaki characterised the electronic identity of the class. His 1958 paper (J. Am. Chem. Soc. 80, 3066) provided the first systematic ultraviolet absorption spectra of the pyrido[1,2-a]pyrimidine series, identifying a constant intense band in the 330–390 nm region (reported as mμ) and explaining it as conjugative interaction with the β-amino-α,β-unsaturated chromophore. The 1962 paper (J. Org. Chem. 27, 1371) went further, isolating the two absorption bands and assigning each to a specific structural feature: a band near 350 nm to the N-substituted pyridone-2-imine chromophore, and a second, separate band near 245 nm to the –C=C–C=O chromophore of the pyrimidinone ring. This distinction — arrived at by systematically testing the effect of substituents and ring-nitrogen substitution on the spectrum across a series of related amidine-derived analogues — became the reference framework the field used to assign structure by UV absorption within the class.

This spectroscopic framework provided a basis for distinguishing the isomeric forms of the ring by ultraviolet absorption.

The 1958 Synthetic Route and Its Patent Record

Antaki's 1958 paper also reported the preparation of 3-acetyl- and 3-cyano-4H-pyrido[1,2-a]pyrimidin-4-ones. The compounds were obtained from 2-aminopyridines and ethyl ethoxymethyleneacetoacetate or ethyl ethoxymethylenecyanoacetate through isolable aminomethylene intermediates, followed by cyclisation under reduced pressure (J. Am. Chem. Soc., 1958, 80, 3066–3069; DOI).

The 1958 paper was later cited in Bristol-Myers patents US 4,122,274 and US 4,209,620 concerning 3-(1H-tetrazol-5-yl)-4H-pyrido[1,2-a]pyrimidin-4-one antiallergic agents. US 4,209,620 states directly: "Some examples of the acrylates of formula VI are disclosed by Antaki in J. Amer. Chem. Soc., 80, 3066 (1958) and by Nishigaki, et al. in J. Heterocycl. Chem., 8, 759 (1971)." The acrylates in question are the uncyclised acrylate precursors to Antaki's 3-cyano pyridopyrimidines — the literature precedent the patent cites for the corresponding starting materials used in its own chemistry. This language was inherited from the parent filing, US 4,122,274, of which US 4,209,620 is a continuation-in-part.

The patents describe converting nitrile-bearing intermediates related to Antaki's chemistry into the corresponding tetrazole derivatives via reaction with an azide salt. The resulting 3-(1H-tetrazol-5-yl)-pyrido[1,2-a]pyrimidin-4-ones are described as inhibitors of allergic reactions, with methods provided for their preparation and pharmaceutical use.

Marketed Medicines on the Ring

The pyrido[1,2-a]pyrimidin-4-one is not a laboratory curiosity. The ring whose structure was settled in 1951 entered human therapy in 1975 and has since supported medicines across four therapeutic areas. Hermecz and Mészáros record that more than a dozen of its derivatives have been introduced into human therapy; the table below lists only those five whose identity we have independently confirmed against a primary source — a patent, an IUPAC name, or a CAS number — rather than the full figure given in the review.

Pemirolast Antiallergic; used ophthalmically for allergic conjunctivitis, with additional indications in some countries Aromatic
Risdiplam (Evrysdi) First approved oral therapy for spinal muscular atrophy Aromatic

That a single core serves an antipsychotic, an antiallergic, an analgesic, and a treatment for a severe genetic neuromuscular disease — in both its aromatic and reduced forms — is what the literature means in calling it a privileged scaffold. Marketed medicines on this ring span half a century, from rimazolium (1975) to risdiplam (2020). Beyond them, a longer set of compounds on this ring (including seganserin, lusaperidone and barmastine) has reached clinical investigation.

Rimazolium (PROBON®), a non-narcotic analgesic marketed in Hungary, was developed at the Hungarian pharmaceutical company CHINOIN by a research group that included István Hermecz and Zoltán Mészáros — the same chemists whose 1983 review of the ring system records that its structure was "first described by Antaki."

Ring identities confirmed against primary sources: risperidone and paliperidone (Janssen patent US 4,804,663); pemirolast (CAS 69372-19-6); risdiplam (IUPAC systematic name, PubChem CID 118513932); rimazolium (PROBON®, CAS 35615-72-6, ATC N02BG02).

The ring system was structurally established by Antaki and Petrow in 1951. The reduction, the substitution, the drug design, and the pharmacology of each medicine are the work of the companies that developed them. What the medicines share is the core ring.

A Second Field: Agrochemicals

The same ring system later reappeared in crop protection. DuPont Crop Protection developed mesoionic derivatives of the pyrido[1,2-a]pyrimidinone framework into the commercial insecticides triflumezopyrim (marketed as Pyraxalt, for rice planthoppers) and dicloromezotiaz (for lepidopteran pests). In describing the class, the DuPont team noted that mesoionic pyrido[1,2-a]pyrimidinones had been "known for several decades" before systematic biological investigation began (Zhang et al., "Mesoionic Pyrido[1,2-a]pyrimidinone Insecticides: From Discovery to Triflumezopyrim and Dicloromezotiaz," Acc. Chem. Res. 2017, 50, 2381–2390; DOI).

The ring whose structure Antaki and Petrow established in 1951 thus underlies marketed products in two separate industries — pharmaceuticals and agrochemicals.

Recognition

Antaki's work on this ring is cited throughout the standard reference literature. The canonical review of the ring system is that of Hermecz and Mészáros (Advances in Heterocyclic Chemistry, Vol. 33, 1983, pp. 241–330; DOI), which credits him as structural corrector, priority figure, and spectroscopic analyst, naming him in the running text. The ring's chemistry is treated again in Comprehensive Heterocyclic Chemistry II (1996), drawing on his 1951, 1958 and 1962 papers across its treatment of structure, spectroscopy and synthesis. His 1958 paper is cited as the primary reference (Reference 1a) for the reagent ethyl ethoxymethyleneacetoacetate in Wiley's Encyclopedia of Reagents for Organic Synthesis (e-EROS).

Medical Relevance

The pyrido[1,2-a]pyrimidin-4-one core underlies five marketed medicines — risperidone, paliperidone, pemirolast, rimazolium, and risdiplam, detailed above — spanning psychiatry, allergy, analgesia, and rare genetic disease, plus two commercial insecticides. This range is what the literature means in calling it a privileged scaffold.

Antiparasitic — a sustained research objective, not a later discovery. Antaki's own research program pursued the scaffold's biological activity directly, not merely its chemistry. His 1962 paper states explicitly: "In continuation of previous work on the schistosomicidal activity in the pyrido[1,2-a]pyrimidine series, the synthesis of some basic derivatives was considered" (J. Org. Chem. 1962, 27, 1371; DOI). New derivatives were synthesised specifically to pursue that activity. Antaki established the ring's structure, characterised its spectroscopic identity, and began investigating its medicinal potential himself, working alone without institutional pharmaceutical backing, well before industrial medicinal chemistry took up the same scaffold for other applications.

The pyrido[1,2-a]pyrimidin-4-one has a documented history of more than a century: a long-standing structural error, its correction, independent confirmation, and a continuing afterlife in medicine and agriculture.
